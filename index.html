01

What CMAAS does in an acquisition

When one company buys another, CMAAS helps make sure the deal is accounted for and reported correctly, from checking the target's accounting before signing to building the combined books after closing.

Put simply, three questions: are the target's numbers what they seem, what are we really paying for, and how will the deal show up in the buyer's financial statements?

Before signing

Is the price built on real numbers?

  • Work alongside PwC's diligence team as they test the target's earnings.
  • Spot accounting policy differences between buyer and target.
  • Flag reporting needs, like audited target financials for an SEC filing.

After closing

Getting the combined books right

  • Purchase accounting: valuing what was bought, and the goodwill left over.
  • The opening balance sheet for the acquired business.
  • Aligning accounting policies, like how revenue is recorded.
  • SEC reporting for the deal, and bringing the target into the buyer's close and controls.

What a first-year actually does

  • Reading. Go through the target's financials and key contracts to spot accounting issues.
  • Bridges. Build the schedule that walks from reported earnings to adjusted earnings.
  • Policy maps. Line up the target's accounting policies against the buyer's and list every difference.
  • Memos. Help draft the accounting memo that explains how the deal is booked.

The deal path

  1. DiligenceAre the numbers real?
  2. SignPrice and terms agreed.
  3. CloseMoney changes hands.
  4. IntegrateOne set of books.

Accounting work runs through every stage

Ten terms to drop naturally

EBITDA

Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. Roughly, the profit a business makes from its operations. Deals are often priced on it.

Multiple

Price divided by EBITDA. Paying 150 million dollars for 10 million of EBITDA is a 15 times multiple, which means paying for 15 years of today's earnings up front, because the price buys the whole company's future earnings, not one year of them. Nobody fixes the number: the market does. You look at what similar companies trade for and what buyers paid in recent deals, then buyer and seller negotiate around those anchors. Faster growth and steadier earnings push the multiple up, risk pushes it down.

Quality of earnings

A diligence review that adjusts reported earnings for one-time items and accounting issues, to find the real, repeatable profit.

Synergy

Extra value from combining two companies. Cost synergies are savings, like sharing factories. Revenue synergies are extra sales, and they're less certain.

Goodwill

The part of the price above the fair value of everything you can identify, like inventory, equipment, and brand names. It's tested for impairment, meaning a write-down, at least once a year.

Purchase price allocation

Splitting the price across the assets and liabilities you bought, at fair value. Whatever is left over is goodwill.

Earnout

Part of the price paid later, only if the business hits agreed targets. It bridges the gap when buyer and seller disagree on value.

Customer concentration

When a few customers make up a big share of sales. If one leaves, revenue drops fast.

Trade promotions

Payments a food brand makes to retailers, like shelf fees and discounts. Under the revenue rules, they usually reduce revenue instead of counting as a marketing expense.

Significant acquisition

When a public company buys a business that's big enough compared with itself, SEC rules require it to file that business's audited financial statements.

The case prompt

Interviewer

Our client is BrightLeaf Foods, a public packaged-snack company with about 500 million dollars in revenue. Its core business is growing only about 2 percent a year.

BrightLeaf wants to buy Sprout Kitchen, a younger plant-based snack brand. Over the last 12 months, Sprout reports about 50 million dollars in revenue, growing 35 percent a year, and 10 million dollars of EBITDA, which is roughly its operating profit. Sprout's founders are asking 150 million dollars.

Your move

Restate the question in one sentence before you do anything else.

Strong answer

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your approach.

Strong questions, with the interviewer's answers

  • Why does BrightLeaf want Sprout?
    Growth. Its core snacks are flat, and plant-based is one of the faster-growing parts of the snack aisle.
  • What do similar deals sell for?
    Fast-growing snack brands have sold for about 12 to 16 times EBITDA. Mature snack companies, like BrightLeaf, trade at about 10 times.
  • Has anyone checked Sprout's numbers?
    Our diligence team just finished a quality of earnings review. We'll show you the results.
  • Have Sprout's financials ever been audited?
    No. A small local firm only reviewed them, which is a much lighter check than an audit.
  • What synergies does BrightLeaf expect?
    About 3 million dollars a year in cost savings from sharing factories and trucks, plus hoped-for extra sales from putting Sprout in BrightLeaf's stores.
  • What does success look like?
    A walk-away price, a recommended offer, and a short list of risks to handle before closing.

Skip questions that don't change your approach, like which flavors Sprout sells. Two or three sharp questions beat six generic ones.

04

Build the framework

Interviewer

Great. How would you structure your approach to this problem?

Your move

Take 60 seconds to sketch three or four buckets, then walk the interviewer through them out loud.

Strong framework

Is $150M a fair price for Sprout, and which accounting risks must be handled before closing?
1Strategic fit
  • Why buy: growth vs. a flat core
  • Fit with BrightLeaf's stores and plants
  • Buy vs. build its own brand
2Quality of the numbers
  • Reported vs. adjusted EBITDA
  • Revenue and policy differences
  • One-time items, customer concentration
3Value and price
  • Standalone value: EBITDA × multiple
  • Synergies, minus integration costs
  • Walk-away price vs. the ask
4Deal risks and plan
  • Audit and SEC reporting needs
  • Purchase accounting, goodwill
  • Terms: price, earnout, conditions

Say it like this

"I'd break this into four parts. First, strategic fit: why BrightLeaf wants Sprout, and whether buying beats building. Second, the quality of Sprout's numbers: what its real, repeatable EBITDA is once we adjust for one-time items and accounting differences. Third, value and price: what Sprout is worth on its own, plus realistic synergies, compared with the 150 million ask. Fourth, deal risks and the plan: audit and SEC reporting needs, purchase accounting, and the terms that protect BrightLeaf. My early guess is that the reported 10 million of EBITDA is too high, so I'd like to start with Sprout's results and the diligence findings."

Why it works

  • It answers both halves of the question: the price, and the risks.
  • The buckets flow: why, what it really earns, what it's worth, how to do the deal safely.
  • It ends with a hypothesis about where to start, which hands the interviewer a natural next step.

If you blank, "fit, numbers, value, risks" is a backup list that works for almost any acquisition case.

05

Exhibit 1: Sprout's growth story

Interviewer

Good place to start. Here are six quarters of Sprout's results. What stands out to you?

Which finding should you lead with?

Revenue jumped to 14 million in Q1 2026, then fell back to 12. That's a launch order to fill a big retailer's shelves, and it won't repeat. The top retailer's share of sales went from 25 percent to about 40 percent. Gross margin slid from 42 percent to 36 percent while trade promotion spend doubled. Repeat purchases are healthy at about 40 percent, so customers like the product. The real question is how much of the growth and profit will last.

Check

Test the claim. Do the quarters add up to the reported 50 million, and is revenue really growing 35 percent a year?

The check

  • The last 12 months are the latest four quarters. Q4 2025 to Q3 2026: 11 + 14 + 12 + 13 = 50 million. That matches the reported 50 million.
  • Older windows are smaller, as they should be. Q2 2025 to Q1 2026 adds to 44 million, and Q3 2025 to Q2 2026 adds to 47 million. Each newer window is bigger: that's what growth looks like.
  • To test the growth rate, compare the same quarter a year apart. Q2: 9 to 12 million, up 33 percent. Q3: 10 to 13 million, up 30 percent. That's a bit below the 35 percent Sprout claims.
  • And the 50 million includes the one-time launch order in Q1 2026, so the repeatable base is a little lower.

"The quarters do add to about 50 million for the last 12 months, but that includes a one-time launch order, and same-quarter growth is closer to 30 percent than 35. A 15 times price assumes fast growth continues, so that gap matters."

Comparing the same quarter a year apart avoids seasonal swings, like a holiday quarter that's always bigger.

Then

Say the insight out loud, tie it to the price, and ask for the next piece of data.

Say it like this

"Customers clearly like Sprout, since repeat purchases are steady at about 40 percent. But three things worry me for the price. The Q1 spike looks like a one-time launch order. One retailer now drives about 40 percent of sales. And margins are slipping as promotion spend rises. So I'd expect the real, repeatable EBITDA to be lower than 10 million. What did the quality of earnings review find?"

Notice the pattern: insight, then so-what for the price, then a hypothesis-driven ask. That ask leads the interviewer to Exhibit 2.

06

Exhibit 2: quality of earnings

Interviewer

Good instinct. Here's the bridge from Sprout's reported EBITDA to what our diligence team thinks is repeatable. What's your read?

What's the so-what?

Three adjustments take EBITDA from 10 million to 8 million: the one-time launch order, a founder salary below what a new CEO would cost, and expired inventory that was never written off. The trade promotion change cuts revenue by 4 million but not EBITDA, because the cost just moves from an expense line to a revenue reduction. Everyday version: if a 4 dollar "sale" was really a 4 dollar discount, your sales go down by 4 dollars, but the money you keep doesn't change. None of this is fraud. It's what a young company's books often look like.

Then

Synthesize both exhibits in three sentences.

Say it like this

"Sprout's real, repeatable EBITDA is about 8 million, not 10, once we remove the launch order, adjust the founder's salary, and write off expired inventory. Its revenue is also about 46 million, not 50, once retailer payments are treated correctly. At 15 times 8 million, Sprout is worth about 120 million on its own, so the 150 million ask needs about 30 million of synergies to make sense."

07

Value it, then size the biggest risk

Same skills as market sizing: state the formula, make clear assumptions, do clean math, then sanity-check. Here you add one risk-sizing step: expected loss equals impact times chance.

Interviewer

*Cost savings are steady, like BrightLeaf's own mature earnings, so value them at BrightLeaf's multiple of about 10. Revenue synergies are too uncertain to pay for upfront.

Your move

Say the formula first: value equals EBITDA times a multiple. Build the walk-away price, then subtract the expected cost of the retailer risk.

  1. 1 · Standalone value$8M × 15$120M
  2. 2 · Net synergy value$3M × 10 − $5M$25M
  3. 3 · Walk-away price, before risk$120M + $25M. Already below the $150M ask$145M
  4. 4 · Impact if the retailer leaves$3M × 15$45M
  5. 5 · Expected loss, and risk-adjusted price$45M × 20% = $9M. $145M − $9M$136M
  6. 6 · The offer$125M upfront + earnout up to $20M. Max $145M, only if the risk doesn't happen$125M + $20M

Quick check: what's the expected cost of the retailer risk?

45 million dollars of lost value times a 20 percent chance is 9 million. 45 million is the impact if it happens, not the expected cost. Everyday version: a 45 dollar concert ticket with a 1 in 5 chance the show gets canceled is worth about 36 dollars to you, not 45.

Standalone = $8M × 15 = $120M Synergies = $3M × 10 − $5M = $25M Risk = $45M × 20% = $9M
Then

Summarize the math in two sentences, and add one sanity check.

Say it like this

"On its own, Sprout is worth about 120 million: 8 million of real EBITDA at 15 times. Cost synergies add about 25 million after integration costs, so the most BrightLeaf should pay is about 145 million, already below the ask, and about 136 million once we allow for the retailer risk. I'd offer about 125 million upfront plus an earnout of up to 20 million, which is still about 16 times real EBITDA, the top of the range for similar deals."

If the interviewer pushes back that the founders won't take less than 150: "Then BrightLeaf should be ready to walk away. Paying 150 means handing all the synergies to Sprout's owners and taking the retailer risk for free. The earnout gets them closer to their number, but only if the business delivers."

08

Score and prioritize on a heat map

Interviewer

Here are the deal risks the team has logged. Score each one for likelihood and impact, from 1 to 5, with one reason for each. Which three should BrightLeaf handle before closing, and why?

Your move

Score each risk out loud with a one-line reason, then name your top three.

The team's scores, with reasons

    Say it like this

    "I'd handle three things before closing. First, the audit: Sprout has never had one, and BrightLeaf may need audited Sprout financials for the SEC soon after closing. Second, the top retailer, at 40 percent of sales, so I'd want a renewed contract or an earnout. Third, the price itself: paying more than about 136 million means booking goodwill that could be written down later. The revenue policy change is certain, but it's a messaging issue, not a value issue."

    Which three come first?

    R2 scores highest: the audit takes months and the SEC deadline doesn't move. R1 and R4 tie next, and they're backed by the exhibits: 40 percent concentration, and a price above every version of the value. R3 is certain but moves revenue, not profit. R5 matters, but BrightLeaf has some time after closing to bring Sprout into its controls. R6 is small next to the price.

    Bonus drills: market sizing and growth

    Two short reps in case the interviewer switches to a classic consulting question.

    Market sizing: plant-based snacks

    Interviewer

    Estimate the yearly U.S. market for plant-based snack brands like Sprout.

    Your move

    Go top-down. Start with households, narrow to buyers, then multiply by what each spends in a year. Say each assumption out loud.

    1. Start broadU.S. households≈130M
    2. × 25% buy plant-based snacks monthlyBuying households≈33M
    3. Spend per household2 packs × $5 × 12 months$120 / yr
    4. Market size33M × $120≈$4B
    5. Sanity checkSprout's $50M ≈ just over 1% share. Fits a young brand✓
    6. Growth check10% a year doubles in about 7 years (72 ÷ 10). Sprout at 35% is taking share✓

    All inputs are interview-style assumptions, not market research.

    Market growth: does it justify the price?

    Interviewer

    Will plant-based snacking keep growing, and what does that mean for the price we pay?

    Your move

    Split growth into more buyers and more spend per buyer, list drivers and headwinds, then link it back to the multiple.

    Strong answer

    Market growth = more buyers × more spend per buyer
    Health-minded shoppers

    More people want snacks with simple, plant-based ingredients.

    Shelf space

    Retailers give growing categories more room, which brings new buyers.

    Younger buyers

    Younger shoppers try new brands more often and stick with ones they like.

    New products

    New flavors and formats raise how much each buyer spends.

    Headwinds

    • Plant-based meat sales fell after an early boom, so hype can fade.
    • Price-sensitive shoppers trade down to cheaper store brands.
    • Big food companies are launching their own plant-based lines.

    Quick math

    Sprout grows 35 percent a year; the market about 10. If Sprout's growth slows to the market's pace, a 15 times multiple looks expensive. That's one more reason to put part of the price in an earnout.

    "I expect the category to keep growing, but more slowly than Sprout is growing today. A 15 times multiple assumes that fast growth continues, so I'd pay for the growth we can see and use an earnout for the growth we're hoping for."
